1. The mini research project

Why do we do a mini research project?

Lectures and textbooks can only take you so far. Doing research shows you things that are hard to appreciate any other way.

You see how ideas become measurable. From the inside, you see how researchers turn abstract ideas into measurable variables: how instructions are worded, questions are designed and responses are recorded. You may also notice how your own expectations shape what you do. These are the issues you’ll need to think critically about in your own designs, such as demand characteristics, operationalisation and measurement validity.

You see ethics in practice. Information sheets, informed consent, the right to withdraw and debriefing make more sense once you’ve implemented them.

You see why sample size and representativeness matter. Your own research will depend on others choosing to take part, and the project gives you a first-hand sense of that.

In short, the mini project helps you become a better researcher by connecting what you learn in class to real research practice.

2. Key readings: Start with these papers to get a better understanding of the main concepts

These papers are essential reading to get started on the research project. Focus on how concepts are defined, operationalised and measured.

Smallwood, J., & Schooler, J. W. (2015). The science of mind wandering: Empirically navigating the stream of consciousness. Annual Review of Psychology, 66, 487–518. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010814-015331

Kaye, L. K., Orben, A., Ellis, D. A., Hunter, S. C., & Houghton, S. (2020). The conceptual and methodological mayhem of “screen time”. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17(10), Article 3661. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17103661

Baumgartner, S. E., Parry, D. A., Beyens, I., Wiradhany, W., Uncapher, M., Wagner, A. D., & Bavelier, D. (2024). The short- and long-term effects of digital media use on attention. In D. A. Christakis & L. Hale (Eds.), Handbook of children and screens: Digital media, development, and well-being from birth through adolescence (pp. 31-37). Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-69362-5_5

Next steps

Once you have gained a foundational understanding of the key concepts mind wandering and screen time from reading the papers mentioned above, search the literature for studies investigating an association between the two. Look for studies that measure the aspects you find most interesting and see what they found. Are the results consistent across studies? If not, what might explain inconsistencies? What do you think is the next step that follows logically from the study or studies that you found?

3. How to write a good research question … and appropriate hypotheses?

Important

Each group will investigate the broad question Is mind wandering associated with screen time.

With your group you need to:

  1. Refine the research topic to hone in on specific aspects of the key concepts of mind wandering and screen time.
  2. Formulate a clear and specific research question.
  3. Write a null hypothesis and alternative hypothesis.

The video below discusses why articulating a good research question is important and how to check that you’ve got one? It also discusses how to write hypotheses to go with your research question. We went over this during the week 6 lab session.

4. Measuring mind wandering and screen time

Important

With your group you need to decide how to measure the variables you’ve specified in your research question. In this module we focus on using questionnaires to measure constructs.

The questionnaire needs to be:

  1. open access (that is, openly available and free to use);
  2. accompanied by a peer-reviewed article that discusses the construction, validity and reliability of the questionnaire.

Below I’ve listed some examples, but you can propose a different questionnaire you have found in the literature as long as it satisfies the above criteria. Asking people to report (a specific aspect of) their screen-time as recorded in a screentime app is also acceptable.

Tip

Fife-Schaw, C. (2020). 14: Questionnaire design. In G. M. Breakwell, D. B. Wright, & J. Barnett (Eds.), Research methods in psychology (5th ed., pp. 119–138). SAGE Publications. Recommended

This chapter provides a useful overview of things to consider when setting up a questionnaire study.

Examples of papers that discuss questionnaires that measure mind wandering

Carriere, J. S. A., Seli, P., & Smilek, D. (2013). Wandering in both mind and body: Individual differences in mind wandering and inattention predict fidgeting. Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology/Revue canadienne de psychologie expérimentale, 67(1), 19–31. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031438

Carriere et al. (2013) capture two forms of mind wandering using the Mind Wandering: Spontaneous (MW-S) and Mind Wandering: Deliberate (MW-D) scales.

Mrazek, M. D., Phillips, D. T., Franklin, M. S., Broadway, J. M., & Schooler, J. W. (2013). Young and restless: Validation of the Mind-Wandering Questionnaire (MWQ) reveals disruptive impact of mind-wandering for youth. Frontiers in Psychology, 4, Article 560. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00560

Mrazek et al. (2013) created a 5-item Mind-Wandering Questionnaire (MWQ) and showed across four studies that people who score higher on it report more task-unrelated thoughts during a reading test.

Schubert, A.-L., Frischkorn, G. T., Sadus, K., Welhaf, M. S., Kane, M. J., & Rummel, J. (2024). The brief mind wandering three-factor scale (BMW-3). Behavior Research Methods, 56(8), 8720–8744. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-024-02500-6

During the first weeks of term, during the lab session you worked with some publicly available data from a research project on mind wandering carried out by Schubert and colleagues (Schubert et al., 2024). They created a 12-item questionnaire that measures how often your mind wanders without meaning to, how often you let it wander on purpose, and how aware you are when it happens.

Examples of papers on measuring aspects of screen time

In addition to the paper by Kaye et al. (2020) referred to in the section with key readings (Section 2), the following papers are good examples of papers that focus on a specific aspect of screen time.

Baumgartner, S. E., Lemmens, J. S., Weeda, W. D., & Huizinga, M. (2017). Measuring media multitasking: Development of a short measure of media multitasking for adolescents. Journal of Media Psychology, 29(4), 188–197. https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-1105/a000167

Baumgartner et al. (2017) developed a short media multitasking measure (MMM-S) – measuring the simultaneous use of two or more media.

Du, J., van Koningsbruggen, G. M., & Kerkhof, P. (2018). A brief measure of social media self-control failure. Computers in Human Behavior, 84, 68–75. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2018.02.002

Du et al. (2018) developed a 3-item scale, the Social Media Self-Control Failure (SMSCF) scale, to measure how often people give in to social media temptations.

Marty-Dugas, J., Ralph, B. C., Oakman, J. M., & Smilek, D. (2018). The relation between smartphone use and everyday inattention. Psychology of Consciousness: Theory, Research, and Practice, 5(1), 46. https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/cns0000131

Marty-Dugas et al. (2018) developed two different measures of smartphone use (general and absent-minded) and explore relationships with measures of everyday inattention.

Ohme, J., Araujo, T., de Vreese, C. H., & Piotrowski, J. T. (2021). Mobile data donations: Assessing self-report accuracy and sample biases with the iOS Screen Time function. Mobile Media & Communication, 9(2), 293-313.

Ohme et al. (2021) present a new methodological approach: mobile data donations using screenshots of the iOS Screen Time function. They assesses its feasibility, sample biases, and the accuracy of self-reported smartphone usage compared to log data in a general population sample of Dutch citizens.
