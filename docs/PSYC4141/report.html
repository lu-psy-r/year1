1. What you are being asked to do?

This term your group designed and ran a study on whether mind wandering is related to screen time. Your task now is to write an individual research report about that study. The report should read like a short journal article: it tells the story of one question, from the reason for asking it, through how you tested it, to what you found and what it means.

You will use the data your group collected, but the analysis and the writing are your own work.

Key information

What do you need to do? Analyse the data from the PSYC4141 research project and write up the results in a research report no longer than 1500 words, plus a 150 word abstract.

When is the deadline? Friday of Week 10 at 12 noon (Friday 11th December 2026)

How do you submit your coursework? You need to submit 2 documents: 1) Submit your report as a Quarto document (.qmd) and your Feedback title sheet as a Word document (.docx) via the submission space on the PSYC4141 Moodle page.

How does the report contribute to your module mark? The mark for the report accounts for 35% of your module mark.

Academic integrity

PSYC4141 is a RED module. This means that you must not use Gen AI tools for any aspect of your work. You will need to confirm that you have not used AI to generate or edit your thoughts/content/words using the AI Transparency Sheet that is part of the Feedback Title sheet. For those students with an ILSP that permits the use of AI tools, you will need to indicate that you have used them and complete an AI transparency appendix as outlined for AMBER and GREEN modules.

2. What the report is about: A chain of alignment

The most important thing we are looking for is not a surprising result. It is a report in which every part connects to the next one. Think of it as a chain:

Introduction Method Results Discussion
idea → research question → hypothesis methods results critical evaluation → take-home message
  • Your introduction explains the idea behind the study and why it matters, leads the reader to your research question, and states your hypothesis: a specific, testable prediction that answers that question.
  • Your method describes how you tested that hypothesis, and you can justify the choices you made.
  • Your results report the analysis that tests the hypothesis, and nothing that wanders off it.
  • Your discussion first evaluates your study critically (who took part, how things were measured, what limits the conclusions), then gives a take-home message that answers the research question using only what the results can support.

Your abstract sits at the start but is written last. It is the whole chain in miniature: a sentence or two on each link, in the same order. If you cannot write it, a link in your report is probably missing.

If one link is weak (for example, a hypothesis that your measures cannot test, or a conclusion that goes beyond your data), the whole report suffers, however well the rest is written. A modest study with a tight chain will score better than an ambitious one with gaps.

3. What your report should contain

Your report follows the structure of a short empirical article:

Section What it does
Title Tells the reader what the study is about
Abstract Summarises the whole study in a short paragraph, following the same chain as the report
Introduction Gives the rationale for the study, ends with your research question and hypothesis
Method Describes participants, measures, procedure and ethics so another researcher could repeat the study
Results Reports the correlation analysis, with a table or figure, and states whether the hypothesis was supported
Discussion Interprets the result, considers who took part and what limits the conclusions, and ends with a clear take-home message
References Lists every source you cite

Detailed guidance on each section, including why APA asks for things to be done in a particular way, is in the separate Section 5.1. The Marking Criteria in Section 4 below show how the report will be marked.

The analysis

You will test your hypothesis using correlation. You will run this analysis yourself in R, inside your Quarto document. You will use the group dataset, but you must run, interpret and report the analysis yourself.

The Quarto document

You submit a single Quarto document (.qmd). It contains your report text and the code that produces your analysis, so that anyone can see exactly how your numbers were produced.

You can use this Quarto template to get started.

  • You write the code for your analysis yourself, using what you have learned in previous weeks. You do not need to learn advanced Quarto features.
  • You type the numbers into your text yourself, reading them from your output. The values you report must come from your output, but you round them as APA requires (the Writing Guidance explains how), so you will not copy every decimal place.
  • Each code block in your document must run without errors.

Your study and your data

Your group:

  • chose how to measure mind wandering and screen time, and submitted a pre-registration with your research question, hypotheses and measures;
  • collected data from about 50 adult participants (recruited from your own networks) through Gorilla.

Your report should be based on your group’s pre-registration, plus the changes you made based on the feedback you received. You may reuse wording from your pre-registration.

The data are shared within your group, and it does not matter if the wording you take from your pre-registration overlaps with that of other students in your group. Everything else, including your analysis and how you explain and interpret your results, must be your own work. Reports that are copied from each other beyond this will be treated as an academic integrity issue.

Word count and formatting

  • The limit is 1500 words. This excludes the title, abstract, figures, tables and reference list. In-text citation are included in the word count. The abstract should be no longer than 150 words.
  • Follow APA 7 for headings, in-text citations, the reference list, and the presentation of statistics, tables and figures.

Before you submit, check that:

  • each part of your report follows from the part before it;
  • your hypothesis can be tested with the measures you used;
  • the numbers in your text come from your output and are rounded and formatted as APA requires;
  • your conclusions do not go further than your data allow;
  • each code block in your Quarto document runs without errors;
  • you are within the word limit and have cited and referenced in APA style.

4. How is your report marked?

Your report is judged on six criteria. For each one, the marker chooses a category: Needs work, Acceptable, Good or Outstanding. Your feedback shows a grid with one tick per criterion, so you can see your profile at a glance, followed by comments on what went well and what to do next time. Your overall mark comes from that profile and the marker’s overall judgement (see Section 4.7 below).

Three things to know:

  • Alignment matters most. The criteria follow the chain from idea to take-home message, and one criterion looks at whether the chain holds across the whole report, including the abstract.
  • Your result does not decide your mark. A weak, zero or unexpected correlation can earn top marks if the report is aligned, honest and well evaluated. A significant result in a report with broken links cannot.
  • Use the grid before you submit. Read each criterion, choose the category you think your report reaches, and find the evidence for it in your own text.

Criterion 1 - Introduction: idea, research question and hypotheses

Category What it looks like
Outstanding Explains why the idea matters, using relevant sources, including a theory or mechanism that could explain why screen time and mind wandering might be associated. Narrows step by step to a research question. States both the null and the alternative hypothesis, which follow directly from that argument, name both variables and state the expected relationship.
Good A clear rationale, question, and testable null and alternative hypotheses. The theory or mechanism is relevant but only partly linked to the hypotheses, or the step from rationale to hypotheses could be tighter.
Acceptable The topic is introduced and a question is stated, but the rationale is general or descriptive, and any theory or mechanism is mentioned rather than explained. The hypotheses are vague, only one is stated, or they do not clearly follow.
Needs work The rationale is missing or unclear, with no theory or mechanism. The question or hypotheses are missing, cannot be tested, or do not match the study.

Criterion 2 - Method

Category What it looks like
Outstanding Another researcher could repeat the study: participants (including the sample size and how they were recruited), measures, procedure and ethics are all clear. Each measure is plainly a measure of a variable in the hypotheses, with a reason for the choice.
Good Complete and clear, including the sample size, with minor gaps. The link between measures and hypotheses is clear but the justification is thin.
Acceptable Most information is there, but gaps would stop someone repeating the study, or the link between measures and hypotheses is left for the reader to work out.
Needs work Key information is missing (such as the sample size), a measure does not match the hypotheses, or the description is too vague to allow replication.

Criterion 3 - Results

Category What it looks like
Outstanding Presents the data to the reader with descriptive statistics for each variable in the text or a table; a figure helps the reader see the pattern. The correlation analysis suits the data, with the checks taught in the module reported briefly. Statistics are reported correctly in APA format. States whether the null hypothesis is rejected or not rejected (never “accepted”) and what this means for the alternative hypothesis. The code runs and produces the reported numbers.
Good Descriptive statistics and a table and/or figure are present, and the analysis is correct, with minor reporting slips. The table or figure could be clearer, or the decision about the null hypothesis is correct but briefly worded.
Acceptable Descriptive statistics are incomplete, or the table and/or figure is missing or hard to follow. The analysis is broadly right, but with errors in reporting or interpretation, or the decision about the null hypothesis is unclear.
Needs work Descriptive statistics are missing or incorrect. The analysis is inappropriate or conducted incorrectly, the numbers in the text do not match the output, there is no decision about the null hypothesis, or the code does not run.

Criterion 4 - Discussion: critical evaluation and take-home message

Category What it looks like
Outstanding Answers the research question first. Relates the finding to key findings in the literature, saying whether it fits them and what might explain any difference. Evaluates this study specifically: who took part and how they were recruited, how each concept was measured, and why a correlation cannot show cause. Explains how each point affects what can be concluded. Ends with a take-home message that goes no further than the data.
Good Answers the question, relates the finding to the literature and evaluates the study sensibly, but the link to the literature is brief, some points are not linked back to the conclusion, or the take-home message is slightly broad.
Acceptable Mostly restates the results, with little or no link to the literature. Limitations are generic (for example “small sample”) and not tied to this study’s conclusion. The take-home message is vague or overreaches.
Needs work No real evaluation, no link to the literature, or conclusions the results do not support (for example, claiming screen time causes mind wandering).

Criterion 5 - Alignment across the report

This criterion is judged on the report as a whole, including the title and abstract.

Category What it looks like
Outstanding Each part follows from the one before: the question fits the idea, the hypotheses answer the question, the methods can test them, the results address them, and the discussion answers the question. The abstract mirrors this chain. A reader could sum up the study in one sentence.
Good The chain holds, with one weak link (for example, a hypothesis that is slightly broader than the measures can test).
Acceptable The chain is visible but has two or more weak links, or one clear mismatch between sections.
Needs work Sections read as separate pieces. The hypotheses, measures, results and conclusion do not address the same question.

Criterion 6 - Scientific writing and APA

Category What it looks like
Outstanding APA 7 is applied accurately and consistently: heading levels and wording, in-text citations, the reference list, the formatting of numbers and statistics, and the numbering and titling of tables and figures. Writing is clear and concise, in a scientific style. The abstract is self-contained. Within the word limit.
Good APA 7 is mostly accurate with a few inconsistencies. Writing is clear, with occasional wordiness or informality.
Acceptable APA 7 is attempted but with repeated errors. Writing sometimes obscures the meaning. The abstract is missing a part of the story.
Needs work APA 7 is largely absent. Writing makes the report hard to follow. Over the word limit, or no abstract.

From criteria to overall mark

The overall mark is a judgement guided by your profile, not an average. As a guide:

Grade Typical profile
A (A+, A, A-) Alignment is Outstanding, at least three other criteria are Outstanding, and none is below Good.
B (B+, B, B-) Alignment is Good or better, most criteria are Good with some Outstanding, and none is below Acceptable.
C (C+, C, C-) Mostly Acceptable and Good. Alignment is at least Acceptable.
D (D+, D, D-) Mostly Acceptable, with some Needs work.
F (F1 to F4) Mostly Needs work, or the hypotheses are not tested. F4 is reserved for no attempt made.

The plus or minus shows how securely the profile fits the grade. Use a plus when the profile sits at the top of the grade (for a B, for example, Alignment is Outstanding or several criteria are Outstanding), a plain letter for a typical fit, and a minus when the profile only just meets the grade (for a B, Alignment is only Good and one criterion is Acceptable).

The big idea: alignment

A research report is an argument. Each part leads into the next one, and the parts have to fit together. We call this alignment. It is the most important thing in your report, and it has its own marking criterion.

Link in the chain The question it answers Where it goes
Idea What are we interested in, and why does it matter? Introduction
Research question What exactly are we asking? Introduction
Hypotheses What do we expect to find, and why? Introduction
Method How did we test it? Method
Results What did we find? Results
Critical evaluation How far can we trust and generalise this finding? Discussion
Take-home message So what does this mean? Discussion

A quick test for alignment: a reader should be able to read just your hypotheses and your take-home message and see that they match. If your introduction promises something that your method doesn’t measure, or your discussion draws a conclusion your results don’t support, the chain is broken.

Suggested word budgets

Introduction ~450 · Method ~300 · Results ~250 · Discussion ~500. This is only a guide, so move words to where your argument needs them.

Title and abstract

Title. Say what you studied in one line, naming both variables, e.g. “Is daily screen time related to mind wandering in young adults?”. Avoid clever puns that hide the topic.

Abstract (about 150 words in a single paragraph, not counted in the word limit). This is a summary of the whole chain in miniature: one or two sentences each for the idea and why it matters, research question, hypotheses, method (including sample size and measures), main result (with the statistic), and take-home message. Write it last.

Tense: use the past tense for what you did and found (“We measured…”, “Screen time was positively correlated with…”). Use the present tense for your conclusion and what it means (“This suggests…”).

Why APA does it this way
  • Standing alone: most readers decide from the title and abstract whether to read on, and databases index them. They need to make sense on their own, so don’t include citations or abbreviations that are only explained later.
  • Tense: your study is finished, so what you did and found are completed events, which take the past tense. Your conclusion is a claim you are making now, so it takes the present tense. The same rule runs through the whole report.

Introduction

The introduction contains the first three links in the chain: idea → research question → hypotheses. Its job is to persuade the reader that your study is worth doing and is designed sensibly. In APA style the introduction has no heading, because the title does that job, so start writing straight after the abstract.

A good structure moves from broad to narrow:

  1. Introduce the concepts. What is mind wandering? How is screen time defined and measured? Use the definitions that match the measures your group chose.
  2. Explain why they might be related. Give a theory or mechanism. For example, how might heavy phone use shape attention, or how might a tendency to mind wander lead people to reach for their phone? Support this with sources.
  3. Summarise what is already known. What have previous studies found? Where are the gaps or inconsistencies?
  4. State your research question. It should follow logically from the gap.
  5. State your hypotheses. Give both the null (H0) and the alternative (H1). Name both variables and say what relationship you expect. If you predict a direction (positive or negative), the introduction must explain why.

Tense: use the present tense for definitions, theories and established knowledge (“Mind wandering is…”, “Attention is a limited resource…”). Use the past tense for what specific earlier studies did and found (“Smith (2020) found…”). Use the past tense for your own hypotheses, because the study has already been carried out (“We hypothesised that…”).

Start from your pre-registration

You don’t need to start from a blank page. Your pre-registration, the feedback on it and your responses to the feedback already contain your research question, hypotheses and the reasons for your decisions. See Building the rationale for your study for an activity that turns these into a plan for your introduction.

Your pre-registration was written before you collected data, so it is in the future tense (“We will measure…”, “We predict…”). Change this to the past tense when you reuse it in your report.

Why APA does it this way
  • Present tense for ideas that are still true: a definition or theory isn’t tied to one moment, so it is written as currently true.
  • Past tense for specific studies: a particular study was carried out at a particular time, so its findings are reported as completed events. This also makes clear to the reader which claims come from one study and which are general knowledge.
  • Citations at the point of the claim: putting the citation right after the idea it supports shows the reader exactly where each claim comes from.
Common pitfalls
  • Describing lots of studies one by one without saying how they build to your question.
  • Hypotheses that appear out of nowhere, with no reason given for the predicted direction.
  • Introducing concepts or measures that never appear in your method.

Method

The method says how you tested your hypotheses. Write it in enough detail that someone else could repeat your study. Use the past tense and these subheadings:

  • Participants: the final sample size, how participants were recruited (e.g. an opportunity sample from your group’s social networks), any inclusion criteria, and key demographics (e.g. age range and mean, gender). If any participants were excluded, say how many and why.
  • Measures: how you measured mind wandering, screen time and any demographics. Name each questionnaire, cite it, and say how many items it has, what the response scale is and how scores were calculated (e.g. the mean of all items, with higher scores meaning more mind wandering). Say why each measure suits the variable in your hypotheses. If you used phone screen time data, explain exactly what was recorded and over what period. Use digits for scale points and scores, even below 10 (e.g. “a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = never to 5 = always”).
  • Procedure: what participants did, in order, and how long it took. Mention that the study ran online on Gorilla and had ethical approval, and say how ethical issues were handled (consent, the right to withdraw, debrief).
Why APA does it this way
  • Detail and subheadings: replication is how science checks its findings. A method section with clear subheadings lets readers find details quickly and judge whether the measures really capture the concepts from your introduction.
  • Past tense: the method is a record of what actually happened (“Participants completed…”). This is the main difference between your method and your pre-registration. The one exception is describing a questionnaire that still exists, which can be in the present tense (“The scale consists of 12 items…”).
How to cite Gorilla?

To cite Gorilla in your research report, please link to the main website www.gorilla.sc. We also recommend stating the date window within which data was collected, so that someone reading the study could cross-reference this with their release notes. Make sure to include the reference below:

Anwyl-Irvine, A. L., Massonnié, J., Flitton, A., Kirkham, N., & Evershed, J. K. (2020). Gorilla in our midst: An online behavioral experiment builder. Behavior Research Methods, 52(1), 388–407. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01237-x

Results

The results report what you found, without interpreting it. Interpretation goes in the discussion.

  1. Descriptive statistics. First things first: you need to present the data to the reader. Report the means and standard deviations for both variables, rounded to two decimal places, in the text, or in a table if you have more than two variables (for example, because you looked at more than one aspect of mind wandering). Number every table and figure (Table 1, Figure 1) and refer to it by number in the text (e.g. “see Figure 1”). Put the number in bold, and the title in italics and title case, above the table or figure. Tables have no vertical lines, and figures need clearly labelled axes, with units.
  2. The inferential test. Illustrate the main finding by including an appropriate figure. A scatterplot is ideal for a correlation. Say which test you used and why (e.g. a Pearson correlation, because both variables are continuous), and report the checks you were taught. Then report the result in APA format, with a 95% confidence interval, e.g. r(48) = .32, 95% CI [.05, .55], p = .023. Put your analysis code in code blocks before or alongside the text that reports it.
  3. Link back to the hypotheses. State whether you reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis (never “accept” it), and what this means for your alternative hypothesis, e.g. “There was a weak, positive, statistically significant correlation between screen time and mind wandering, so the null hypothesis was rejected in favour of the alternative hypothesis.”

Tense: use the past tense for your findings (“Mind wandering was positively correlated with…”). Use the present tense when pointing the reader to a table or figure, because it is in front of them now (“Table 1 shows…”).

Why APA does it this way
  • Past tense for results: your results describe what happened in your sample, at the time you collected the data. Writing “was correlated” rather than “is correlated” also stops you claiming more than your data show.
  • Statistical symbols are in italics (M, SD, N, r, p) so they stand out from the text. N is the whole sample and n is part of it.
  • Spaces around =, < and > make statistics easier to read, just as spaces between words do.
  • Words or digits: small numbers read more naturally as words in a sentence. Digits are used wherever precision matters, such as data, measurements and scales, so readers can quickly spot and compare the values that carry your evidence.
  • Digits for all statistics: values such as M = 3.42 and r = .32 are always written in digits, however small. If a sentence would start with a number, rephrase it.
  • No leading zero for r and p (.32, not 0.32), because these values can never be greater than 1. Values that can be greater than 1 keep the zero (M = 0.85), so the missing zero tells the reader something.
  • Degrees of freedom go in brackets. For a correlation, df = N − 2. This lets readers check your sample size.
  • Rounding: r to two decimal places, p to three. For means and standard deviations for integer-scale data (e.g., surveys): one decimal, and two decimals for other means and SDs. Your output shows many more digits, but they suggest a precision your data don’t have.
  • Exact p values give more information than “p < .05”. Use p < .001 for very small values.
  • The confidence interval shows the range of plausible values for the true correlation, so readers can see how precise your estimate is, not just whether it is significant.

See the APA Style Guide - Numbers and Statistics for full guidance.

Your code and your numbers must agree

Your Quarto document has to include the code for your descriptive statistics, your figure or table, and your correlation. Every number you write in the text must match what your code produces. Check them carefully before you submit.

Discussion

The discussion contains the last two links in the chain: critical evaluation → take-home message. It moves from narrow back to broad, so it mirrors your introduction.

  1. Answer your research question first, in plain words and without repeating the statistics, and say whether the finding supports your hypothesis.
  2. Relate it to the literature. Is it consistent with the studies and the mechanism from your introduction? If not, why might that be?
  3. Critically evaluate. Focus on the issues that matter for your conclusions:
    • Who you recruited. How might a sample drawn from your own social networks affect the relationship you found and who it generalises to?
    • Measurement. How well do your measures capture the concepts? For example, how accurate is self-reported screen time?
    • Correlation is not causation. Could screen time affect mind wandering, mind wandering affect screen time, or could a third variable affect both?
    For each limitation, explain how it affects your conclusion and suggest a specific way future research could address it.
  4. Finish with a take-home message. What should the reader remember? It should answer your research question and match your hypotheses.

Tense: use the past tense when restating your findings and those of other studies (“We found…”, “Jones (2021) also reported…”). Use the present tense for interpretation, implications and your take-home message (“This suggests…”, “Future research should…”).

Why APA does it this way
  • Narrow to broad: the discussion mirrors the introduction, so the reader ends where they started, with the bigger question, and can see what your study has added.
  • Switching tense: the past tense marks what was observed, and the present tense marks what you are claiming it means. Keeping the two apart lets the reader see where your evidence ends and your interpretation begins, which is the core of critical evaluation.

::: {.callout-warning title=“Common pitfalls} - A generic list of limitations (”the sample was small”) with no explanation of why it matters for this finding. - Causal language (“screen time causes mind wandering”) about a correlational study. - New results, or ideas that were never introduced, appearing only in the discussion. :::

References and APA style

Headings: use the headings in the template. Method, Results, Discussion and References are main headings (APA Level 1, written with #). Participants, Measures and Procedure are subheadings (APA Level 2, written with ##). Write headings in title case, and don’t add other main headings.

Citations in the text follow APA 7th edition:

  • One author: Smith (2020) found…, or … (Smith, 2020).
  • Two authors: Smith and Jones (2020) found…, or … (Smith & Jones, 2020). Use “and” in the sentence and “&” inside brackets.
  • Three or more authors: Smith et al. (2020) found…, or … (Smith et al., 2020).
  • Paraphrase rather than quote. Give a page number only for a direct quote.

Reference list: list every source in APA 7 format, in alphabetical order by the first author’s surname. Every source cited in the text must be in the reference list, and the other way round. APA uses a hanging indent, but you do not need to create one in your Quarto document. The pattern for a journal article is:

Author, A. A., & Author, B. B. (Year). Title of the article in sentence case. Name of the Journal in Title Case, volume(issue), first page–last page. https://doi.org/xxxxx

Scientific writing is clear, concise and precise. Use one idea per paragraph, start each paragraph with a topic sentence, define terms before using them, and use the same name for a variable throughout. You may use “we” for decisions your group made together.

Numbers: write numbers below 10 in words (“three questionnaires”, “five groups”) and numbers 10 and above in digits (“12 items”, “48 participants”). There are some important exceptions. Always use digits for:

  • statistics, decimals, percentages and ratios (M = 3.42, 25%)
  • numbers with a unit of measurement or time (“2 hours of screen time”, “5 min”)
  • ages, scores and points on a scale (“aged 19”, “a 5-point scale”, “rated from 1 to 7”)

Never start a sentence with a digit. Either write the number in words (“Forty-eight participants took part.”) or rephrase the sentence (“The sample included 48 participants.”).

Tense at a glance

Section Past tense for… Present tense for…
Abstract what you did and found your conclusion
Introduction specific earlier studies; your hypotheses definitions, theories, established knowledge
Method everything you and participants did describing a questionnaire that still exists
Results your findings pointing to tables and figures
Discussion your findings and those of other studies interpretation, implications, take-home message

For more info see the APA Style Guide - Tense

Why APA does it this way
  • Heading levels: consistent heading levels show the structure of the report at a glance, so readers can find any section in any APA paper.
  • Alphabetical order and hanging indent: the author’s surname stands out, so readers can quickly find the full reference for any in-text citation.
  • “et al.” for three or more authors: this keeps in-text citations short, while the reference list gives every author’s name in full.

For further guidance see the APA Style Guide - References
