1. Week 1 - Being a scientist, doing a science

Written by Rob Davies and Margriet Groen

Next, you can see the lecture elements. We recommend that you watch them in sequence.

Reading

Easy access to readings

All reading is accessible via the library. You can go to Moodle -> Library Resources -> Resource List or you can follow this link. Readings have one of the following tags:

  • Essential: We expect you to be familiar with these reading. You will be assessed on this.
  • Recommended: We highly recommend these readings. They often provide a different perspective, or explain things in a different way.
  • Optional: Further readings for those interested.

The reading list is divided into multiple sections:

  • Reading associated with statistics: this contains links to readings useful for the development of statistical concepts and skills. The main textbook Fundamental statistics for the behavioral sciences by Howell (Essential) lives here.
  • Reading associated with psychological research principles and design: this contains links to readings that provide a good basis for foundational knowledge around research design.
  • Reading associated with Open Science principles and practices: this contains links to key literature on the Open Science movement.
  • Week by week: these contain links to key reading for that week that are not listed on any of the more general lists above.

For week 1, the reading is:

  • McMillan, B. T. M., Baumgartner, H.A., Bergmann, C. et al. (2026). What 5000 babies can tell us about developing minds and how to study them. Communications Psychology, 4, 1-5. Essential
  • Peterson, D. (2016). The baby factory: Difficult research objects, disciplinary standards, and the production of statistical significance. Socius, 2, 1-10. Recommended
  • Wright, D. B., Barnett, J., & Breakwell, G. M. (2020). Psychology as a science: What is science? In G. M. Breakwell, D. B. Wright, & J. Barnett (Eds.), Research methods in psychology (5th ed., pp. 119–138). SAGE Publications. Recommended

Lab activities

Lab activity 1: Reading in data and creating a plot

Background

We’ve all been there: you may be trying to focus on a lecture, but a thought on what you would like to have for lunch pops into your mind. Mind wandering describes the common experience of one’s thoughts drifting away from the task at hand towards self-generated thoughts and feelings (Smallwood & Schooler, 2006). Today, we’ll work with some publicly available data from a research project on mind wandering. Anna Schubert and colleagues (Schubert et al., 2024) conducted a study in which they created a 12-item questionnaire that measures how often your mind wanders without meaning to, how often you let it wander on purpose, and how aware you are when it happens. You can have a look at the questionnaire here.

Aims of the activity

We are going to:

  1. Download the files
  2. Setup the appropriate bits and pieces in RStudio
  3. Read in the data
  4. Build a plot
Watch the ‘How to’ lecture

If you haven’t done so already, this is a good time to watch the ‘How to’ video (here Section 1.3) on ‘How to build a plot’.

Step 1: Download the files

To complete this lab activity you will need two files:

  1. We’ll work with an example script called 4141_wk1_lab_act1.qmd. This is a Quarto document.
  2. We need a data file called data_bmw3.csv. This is a file that contains the responses of the participants in one of the studies reported on in the paper by Schubert et al. (2024). It is a file of the type .csv. This means that individual bits of data are separated by comma’s. We’ll often use .csv files.

You can download the files here: 4141_wk1_lab.zip. Usually they will be saved in your ‘Downloads’ folder.

Next, upload the zip-folder as it is to your Files space on the R-server (bottom right quadrant; no need to extract the files first).

Finally, open the example script by clicking on the file called 4141_wk1_lab_act1.qmd in the Files space on the R-server.

Steps 2, 3 and 4

For steps 2, 3 and 4, you’ll be working in RStudio.
