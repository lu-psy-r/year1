2. Week 2 - Measuring, describing, and visualising psychological concepts

Written by Carly Anderson and featuring lectures from John Towse

Overview

This week we focus on two strands of learning:

  1. Psychological measurement - how we turn psychological concepts into measurable data, and how we can summarise those data using numbers.
  2. Variability and distributions - why differences between people and observations matter, and how we can measure and visualise information that a single average might hide.

The materials we have prepared to support your learning are arranged in four parts:

  • Lectures
  • Reading
  • Pre-lab activities
  • Lab activities

Lectures

Last week, you looked at some of the bigger questions that sit behind psychological science: how theories are linked to observable evidence, why our observations provide estimates rather than direct access to the “truth”, and why the complexity and variability of human behaviour matter.

This week, we move from those ideas to some of the practical tools that allow us to gather and make sense of evidence. We’ll look at how psychological concepts can be represented as data, and how choices about measurement, summary and visualisation shape the evidence we use to evaluate scientific claims.

The lectures are grouped into section A and B (relating to the two strands of learning stated above). Each section is split into short videos so you can work through them at your own pace. It’s a good idea to take notes as you go and to have a 10-minute break between section A and section B.

Pre-lab activity 1: From psychological claims to measurements

Last week, you considered psychological claims that we might encounter in everyday life. One example is the claim that “screen time negatively impacts mental health in adolescents”. Before we could investigate this claim, we would need to decide what we actually mean by screen time and how we could turn that concept into something we can observe and measure.

One simple approach might be to ask people: “Approximately how many hours did you spend looking at a screen yesterday?”

Imagine we asked a small group of adolescents and received the following sample of responses:

6 8 5 9 7 4 11 6 7 8 10 5 6 12 7 8 4 9 6 7 14 5 8 6 10 7 3 9

Take a moment to think about what these numbers actually represent.

Pre-lab question 1
  1. What scale of measurement best describes the number of hours of screen time reported here?
  1. Nominal
  2. Ordinal
  3. Interval
  4. Ratio
Show answer

d. Ratio. Time measured in hours has equal intervals, and zero hours represents the absence of the measured quantity. This means that differences and ratios between values are meaningful.

Once we have collected observations like these, how can we describe them in a useful way? One way is to summarise the data to find the mean or average value. You’ll learn how to do that in RStudio during the lab, but for now, have a look at the website app: Describing and Exploring Quantitative Variables: https://istats.shinyapps.io/EDA_quantitative/. The website should open in a new window. You may be offered a free trial of the ‘Art of Stat Mobile App’; you can dismiss that.

Choose the following options:

  • Under ‘Enter Data’, select ‘Your Own’
  • Under ‘Name of Variable’, enter a sensible label (such as ‘Screen time’)
  • Under ‘Enter observations’, add the numbers of the observations
  • Under ‘Choose Type of Plot’, click the box to select a ‘Histogram’.

Now have a look:

  • On the right, you’ll see key descriptive statistics such as the mean and the standard deviation (Std. Dev.).
  • Below it, you can see a histogram. If the histogram looks funny, try adjusting the bin size.
What is a histogram?

A histogram is a graph that shows how the values of a continuous (or finely measured) variable are distributed. The values are divided into consecutive, equal-width intervals called bins, and a bar is drawn for each bin with its height showing how many observations fall inside it (or the proportion or density). Because the bins are adjacent, the bars touch, which distinguishes a histogram from a bar chart of separate categories. Histograms make it easy to see the shape of a distribution, including its centre, spread, skew, and any outliers or multiple peaks.

Play around:

  • What happens if you add more observations? How does it change the mean and the standard deviation? What happens to the histogram?
  • Try adding 10 observations of lower screen time values.
  • Try adding an impossibly large observation, like 89. That is, of course, far larger than there are hours in the day, but data entry errors like that do happen. How does it effect the mean and the standard deviation?
  • You can also click the option to see a boxplot as well as a histogram. Can you work out how key descriptive statistics are represented in a boxplot?
Pre-lab question 2
  1. The mean reported screen time in this sample was approximately 7.4 hours. Which of the following would NOT be justified from these data?
  1. Participants reported an average of 7.4 hours of screen time.
  2. There was variation in reported screen time between participants.
  3. Adolescents who use screens more have poorer mental health.
  4. Some participants reported more screen time than others.
Show answer

c. Here, our measure of screen time is self-reported hours of screen use on one day. We have not measured mental health, so these observations cannot tell us whether greater screen use is associated with poorer mental health. Our measure of “screen time” is also only one possible way of representing a complex behaviour. For example, it does not distinguish between different activities or content, such as studying, gaming, messaging friends or watching videos. This illustrates an important challenge in psychological science: the questions and theories we are interested in are often complex, while the data we collect are particular observations produced by the choices we make about what and how to measure. So, even when we can accurately describe a set of observations, we still need to be careful about what those observations allow us to conclude.
