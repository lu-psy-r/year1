8. Week 8 - Developing the linear model

Written by Rob Davies and Samuel Finnerty

Overview

This week we focus on two strands of learning:

  1. The linear model with multiple predictors

Last week you learned about regression and the linear model as a way of predicting one variable from knowledge of one or more other variables.

This week you will learn about multiple regression which involves the linear model you learned last week but this time with multiple predictors i.e., as a way of predicting one variable from knowledge of more than one variable.

Regression is really useful when we have more than one predictor which is why we will be covering multiple regression this week and in week 9. This week, we will also include a categorical variable (education level) as a predictor in a linear model.

We will do all this in the context of the Clearly Understood project on how easy or difficult people find it to understand written health information.

  1. Research project: How to write a report

We will also continue the group project by focussing on data collection and how to write a report.

Lectures

Note

Please note that Rob may mention a different module code or different week during the lectures. I’ve reviewed the material and the content is entirely relevant to the current module.

Tip

In week 8, we aim to further develop skills in analysing and in visualising psychological data. We will do so in the context of the Clearly Understood project.

We will aim to answer the research question: What person attributes in adult readers predict success in understanding written health information?

Research questions like this can be examined using linear models.

When we do these analyses, we will need to adapt how we report the results:

  • we need to report information about the model we specify, identifying all predictors;
  • we will need to decide if the effects of one or more predictors are significant;
  • we will report the model fit statistics (F, R-squared) as well as coefficient estimates;
  • and we need to learn to write texts describing the impact of predictors.

Usually, in describing the impacts of predictors, we are required to communicate:

  • the direction of the effect – do values of the outcome variable increase or decrease given increasing values of the predictor?
  • the size of the effect – how much do values of the outcome variable increase or decrease given increasing values of the predictor?

This task of description is enabled by producing plots of the predictions we can make:

  • plots to show we expect the outcome to change, given different values of a predictor.

The lectures have three main areas of focus

1. Working with the linear model with multiple predictors

We focus in-depth on how you code linear models, how you identify critical information in the results summaries, and how you report the results: the language and the style you can use in your reports.

Tip
  • A small change to lm() coding releases tremendous power and flexibility in how you use the analysis method.

2. Analyses are done in context so when we conduct analyses we must use contextual information

The power and flexibility of the linear model presents challenges. We must decide which predictor variables we specify in our model. This specification requires us to think about our theoretical assumptions and what they require us to include to make sense of the behaviours or the individual differences we observe when we do things like investigating what makes health information easy or difficult to understand.

3. Developing critical thinking

As we develop conceptual understanding and practical skills, we must learn to reflect critically on our analyses, and learn to critically evaluate the analyses we read about when we read research reports in the scientific literature.

Tip

Critical analysis can develop by considering

  • validity
  • measurement
  • generalizability

We are always working in the broader context of uncertainty:

  • uncertainty about the predictions we may make concerning outcomes of interest;
  • uncertainty given the possibility that predicted effects may vary between individuals or groups;
  • uncertainty given the influence of sources of randomness in how specific responses are produced.

Step 4: Revise – fit a linear model with one predictor

We start by revising how to use lm() with one predictor: the model of mean.acc and HLVA that you fitted in Week 7.

Action: In a new code chunk, use lm() to fit a model with mean.acc as the outcome and HLVA as the predictor. Then get the model summary.

Code 
model.1 <- lm(mean.acc ~ HLVA, data = study.two.gen)
summary(model.1)

In Week 7, we looked at the coefficient estimates. This week, we also look at the information at the bottom of the model summary:

  • Adjusted R-squared tells us how much of the variation in the outcome the model accounts for, as a proportion between 0 and 1.
  • F-statistic tests whether the model as a whole predicts the outcome better than a model with no predictors. It is reported with two degrees of freedom (DF) and a p-value.

Questions Step 4

  1. What is the estimate for the coefficient of HLVA, and what does it mean in words?
  2. Report the F-statistic (F, the two DF values and the p-value) and the Adjusted R-squared.
  3. Explain in words what this R-squared value indicates.

Step 5: Fit a linear model with multiple predictors

Now we fit a model that includes all of these predictors at the same time: health literacy (HLVA), vocabulary knowledge (SHIPLEY), reading strategy (FACTOR3) and age in years (AGE). This is the same model as the one presented in the lectures.

Action: In a new code chunk, use lm() to fit a model with mean.acc as the outcome and HLVA, SHIPLEY, FACTOR3 and AGE as the predictors. Give the model a new name. Then get the model summary. Try to write the code yourself before you look at the Code below.

How do we add more predictors?

We list the predictor variables on the right of the tilde ~, separated by a plus sign +:

model <- lm(outcome ~ predictor.1 + predictor.2 + predictor.3, data = data.set)
summary(model)
  • The model summary now has one row in the Coefficients table for each predictor.
  • Each coefficient estimate shows the predicted change in the outcome for a one-unit increase in that predictor, while holding the other predictors constant.
Code 
model.2 <- lm(mean.acc ~ HLVA + SHIPLEY + FACTOR3 + AGE, data = study.two.gen)
summary(model.2)

  3. Compare the Adjusted R-squared with the model in Step 4. What does the difference tell you?
  4. Does the AGE estimate suggest that older or younger participants were more accurate? Is the effect significant?

  • For question b: health literacy and vocabulary knowledge are both measures of language experience. How might they be related to each other? You can check the correlation between them using correlation(), as you did in Week 7.

Step 6: Report the results of a linear model with multiple predictors

When we report a linear model with multiple predictors, we report the model we specified (the outcome and all the predictors), the model fit statistics (F with its degrees of freedom and p-value, and Adjusted R-squared) and, for each predictor, the estimate, t, p and the direction of the effect.

Action: In your Quarto document, write a short report of the model you fitted in Step 5. Write it as plain text, not in a code chunk. Use the example in the lectures and the template in the Hint.

Hint

Fill in the gaps ... using the model summary from Step 5.

We fitted a linear model with mean comprehension accuracy as the outcome and health literacy (HLVA), vocabulary knowledge (SHIPLEY), reading strategy (FACTOR3) and age (years) as predictors. The model is significant overall, F(…, …) = …, p …, and explains …% of variance (adjusted R² = …). The model estimates showed that the accuracy of comprehension increased with higher levels of … (b = …, t = …, p …), …

  • Report the estimate as b. In the lecture slides the estimate is written as β. In APA style, β is normally used for standardised estimates, and the estimates from lm() are unstandardised, so we use b.
  • Round estimates, t and R-squared to two or three decimal places.
  • When R shows a very small p-value (e.g. 2.87e-12), report it as p < .001.

Step 7: Plot model predictions

In Week 7, we used geom_abline() to draw a prediction line from the intercept and slope of a model with one predictor. With multiple predictors, the prediction depends on the values of all the predictors, so we use ggpredict() from the {ggeffects} library. It works out the predicted outcome across the range of one predictor, while holding the other predictors constant at their average (mean) values.

Action 1. Use ggpredict() to get the predictions for HLVA from the model you fitted in Step 5. Print the predictions by typing the name you gave them, then plot them with plot(). Do the same for SHIPLEY.

Code 
dat.HLVA <- ggpredict(model.2, "HLVA")
dat.HLVA
plot(dat.HLVA)

dat.SHIPLEY <- ggpredict(model.2, "SHIPLEY")
dat.SHIPLEY
plot(dat.SHIPLEY)
  • ggpredict(model.2, "HLVA") tells R which model to use (model.2) and which predictor we want predictions for ("HLVA", in quotation marks).
  • The printed predictions show, under Adjusted for:, the values the other predictors are held at.
  • In the plot, the line shows the predicted mean.acc and the shaded band shows the 95% confidence interval.
  • Optional: make prediction plots for FACTOR3 and AGE in the same way.

plot() zooms the y-axis in on the range of the predictions, so each plot has a different y-axis. This can make a small effect look large.

Action 2. plot() produces a ggplot, so you can edit it like your other plots. Edit the HLVA plot using theme_bw(), labs() and ylim(), setting the y-axis to the full possible range of mean.acc (0-1).

Code 
plot(dat.HLVA) +
  theme_bw() +
  labs(x = "Health literacy (HLVA)", y = "Predicted mean accuracy", title = "") +
  ylim(0, 1)

R will show the message Scale for y is already present. Adding another scale for y, which will replace the existing scale. This is not an error: it tells you that ylim() has replaced the y-axis that plot() set.

Questions Step 7

  1. Use the HLVA estimate from Step 5 to work out how much predicted accuracy changes between an HLVA score of 4 and a score of 14. Check your answer against the printed predictions.
  2. The estimate for SHIPLEY is smaller than the estimate for HLVA. Does this mean that vocabulary knowledge matters less than health literacy? Compare the two prediction plots, and check their axes.

Step 8: Draw boxplots to compare groups

Next, we look at a categorical variable: education level (EDUCATION). Participants reported their education as Further (further education), Higher (higher education) or Secondary (secondary school). A boxplot shows the distribution of a numeric outcome for each group: the middle line is the median, and the box holds the middle 50% of scores.

Action: In a new code chunk, draw boxplots of mean.acc for each level of EDUCATION, with each participant’s score shown as a point.

Code 
ggplot(data = study.two.gen, aes(x = EDUCATION, y = mean.acc)) +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.2, height = 0, alpha = 0.5) +
  geom_boxplot(outlier.shape = NA, colour = "red", alpha = 0.1) +
  theme_bw() +
  labs(x = "Education level", y = "Mean accuracy") +
  ylim(0, 1)
  • The x variable must be categorical (EDUCATION) and the y variable numeric (mean.acc).
  • geom_jitter() draws a point for each participant, moved slightly left or right so that points do not sit on top of each other. height = 0 keeps each point at the participant’s real score.
  • outlier.shape = NA stops geom_boxplot() drawing outlying scores a second time, because they are already shown as points.
  • See the reference pages for geom_boxplot() and geom_jitter().

Questions Step 8

  1. Compare the medians of the three groups, and the number of participants in each group. Why might the group sizes matter?
  2. Optional: draw the same plot for ETHNICITY. What does it show you about the participant sample?

Step 9: Fit a linear model with a categorical predictor

lm() can include categorical variables as predictors, added to the model in exactly the same way as numeric variables.

Action 1. Fit a model with mean.acc as the outcome and HLVA, SHIPLEY, FACTOR3, AGE and EDUCATION as predictors. Give the model a new name. Then get the model summary.

Code 
model.3 <- lm(mean.acc ~ HLVA + SHIPLEY + FACTOR3 + AGE + EDUCATION, data = study.two.gen)
summary(model.3)
How does R handle a categorical predictor?
  • R picks one of the categories (levels) to be the reference (baseline) level. Unless you tell it otherwise, it picks the level whose name comes first in the alphabet: here, Further.
  • The model summary has a row for each of the other levels: EDUCATIONHigher and EDUCATIONSecondary.
  • Each estimate is the predicted difference in the outcome between that level and the reference level, holding the other predictors constant.

There are other ways to code categorical predictors, which you will meet in later years.

Action 2. Use ggpredict() and plot() to plot the predicted effect of EDUCATION.

Code 
dat.EDUCATION <- ggpredict(model.3, "EDUCATION")
plot(dat.EDUCATION)

For a categorical predictor, the plot shows a dot for the predicted mean.acc of each group, and whiskers (lines) showing the 95% confidence interval: longer lines mean more uncertainty. The 95% refers to the procedure, not to one particular interval: if we repeated the study over and over, 95% of the intervals calculated in this way would contain the true value (Hoekstra et al., 2014).

Questions Step 9

  1. Which level of EDUCATION is the reference level?
  2. What does the estimate for EDUCATIONSecondary mean? Is it significant?
  3. In the plot, which group has the longest whiskers? Why do you think this is?
  4. Stretch question: in the boxplot in Step 8, the Secondary group looked noticeably less accurate than the other two groups. Why is the difference estimated in the model smaller?
Tip

You can read more about confidence intervals here:

Hoekstra, R., Morey, R. D., Rouder, J. N., & Wagenmakers, E. J. (2014). Robust misinterpretation of confidence intervals. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 21, 1157-1164.

Step 10: Extension – Can people accurately evaluate whether they understand written health information?

Important

The second research question in the Clearly Understood project is:

  1. Can people accurately evaluate whether they correctly understand written health information?

Participants also rated how well they thought they understood each health text, on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 9 (very well), which gave us their mean.self scores. If people can evaluate their own understanding, their ratings should be associated with their tested accuracy (mean.acc).

Action 1. Fit a model with mean.self as the outcome and mean.acc as the predictor. Then fit a second model the other way round: mean.acc as the outcome and mean.self as the predictor. Give each model a different name and get the summaries.

Code 
model.self <- lm(mean.self ~ mean.acc, data = study.two.gen)
summary(model.self)

model.acc <- lm(mean.acc ~ mean.self, data = study.two.gen)
summary(model.acc)

Action 2. Use ggpredict() and plot() to plot the predictions from each model.

Code 
dat.self <- ggpredict(model.self, "mean.acc")
plot(dat.self)

dat.acc <- ggpredict(model.acc, "mean.self")
plot(dat.acc)

Questions Step 10

  1. In the model with mean.self as the outcome, is mean.acc a significant predictor? What does this suggest is the answer to research question 2?
  2. Compare the two models. Why are the slope estimates so different, when the variables and the data are the same? What do you notice about the t values, p-values and R-squared values?
Hint
  • mean.acc is scaled from 0 to 1 (the proportion of questions answered correctly) and mean.self is scaled from 1 to 9 (the average rating).
  • A coefficient estimate is the predicted change in the outcome for a one-unit increase in the predictor. What does a one-unit increase mean for each of the two variables?
