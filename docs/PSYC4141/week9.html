9. Week 9 - The linear model (with interaction)

Written by Rob Davies, Samuel Finnerty, & Margriet Groen

NoteOverview

This week we focus on two strands of learning:

  1. The linear model with interactions

In Weeks 7 and 8 you learned how to use the linear model to predict one variable from one or more other variables, including a categorical variable.

This week you will learn how to test whether the effect of one predictor is different for different values of another predictor. This is called an interaction.

Psychologists often expect effects to vary between groups of people, or for people with different scores on a third variable. Interactions let us examine this.

We will do all this in the context of the Clearly Understood project on how easy or difficult people find it to understand written health information.

Lectures

Note

Please note that Rob may mention a different module code or different week during the lectures. I’ve reviewed the material and the content is entirely relevant to the current module.

Tip

In week 9, we aim to further develop skills in analysing and in visualising psychological data. We will do so in the context of the Clearly Understood project.

We will face what the lectures call the real challenge in psychological science:

  1. People vary
  2. Effects vary
  3. Results vary

Because the effects that interest us can be expected to vary, we need to be able to investigate how they vary. We do this using linear models that include interaction effects.

  • If we think that the shape or size of an effect is different in different groups of people, or for different values of a third variable (a moderator),
  • then we are thinking about an interaction effect.

When we report a model that includes an interaction, we still report the model we specified, the model fit statistics (F, R-squared) and the coefficient estimates. We now also need to describe how the effect of one predictor changes, given different values of another predictor. Plots of model predictions help us to do this.

The lectures have four main areas of focus

1. The real challenge in psychological science

We connect (1.) the fact that psychological and social processes vary to (2.) the uncertainties we face when we try to draw conclusions from limited samples.

Tip

What makes psychological science challenging:

  1. People vary
  2. Effects vary
  3. Results vary

2. Critical thinking

So far, we have looked at the impact on understanding of differences between individuals in attributes like vocabulary knowledge. Now, we consider the possibility that the effect of one predictor could itself vary.

Tip

We can develop our critical thinking skills by considering:

  1. What effects can vary
  2. How effects can vary

We discuss the kinds of theoretical assumptions that may lead psychologists to hypothesise interaction effects.

3. Coding models with interactions

We build on what we know about coding linear models with multiple predictors to code models that include interaction effects.

Tip

We build skills by looking at:

  • the code we use;
  • the key information in the results, where to find it, and how to interpret it;
  • the different ways in which we can present and interpret interactions.

4. Interactions between numeric variables and factors

The effect of a numeric variable (like reading strategy) can differ depending on another numeric variable (like health literacy). An effect can also differ between groups, which we code using categorical variables (factors) like education level. We look at how to code, interpret and report interactions between numeric variables and factors.

Pre-lab activities

After having watched the lectures you’ll be in a good position to try these activities. Completing them before you attend your lab session will help you to consolidate your learning and help move through the lab activities more smoothly.

Pre-lab activity 1: Predict before you look

In the lectures, Rob describes two theories about how the effect of health literacy on understanding might depend on vocabulary knowledge. In the lab, we ask a similar question about reading strategy:

Is the effect of reading strategy (FACTOR3) on accuracy of understanding (mean.acc) different for people with different levels of health literacy (HLVA)?

Here are two possible theories:

  • Theory 1: the effect of reading strategy is larger (the slope is steeper) for people with higher health literacy, because people who know more about health can make more use of a good reading strategy.
  • Theory 2: the effect of reading strategy is smaller (the slope is shallower) for people with higher health literacy, because people who already understand health information well have less room to improve.

Before the lab, write down your predictions:

  1. Which theory do you think is more likely? Why?
  2. Do you expect the effect of reading strategy to be different for people with further education and people with higher education? If so, in which direction?

Keep your predictions: you will compare them with the results in Steps 5 and 6 of the lab.

Tip

In the lab, we include education level (EDUCATION) as a categorical predictor, as we did in Week 8. If you need to, look back at Step 9 of the Week 8 lab to remind yourself what a reference level is.

Pre-lab activity 2: See how slope estimates vary between samples

Have a look at this app on sampling variation in linear models by Rob Davies.

The app simulates data for a linear model with one predictor. It shows the slope estimate for one sample, alongside four more samples drawn from the same population, and two piles of 100 simulated studies: one where there is no effect in the population, and one where there is an effect.

Important

The app runs in Chrome or Edge, not in Safari. The first time you open it, it downloads the software it needs (60-90 MB), which can take up to a minute.

Try the following:

  1. Compare the slope estimates in the different samples from the same population. Click Draw new samples a few times.
  2. Change N per study to make the samples smaller, then larger, and draw new samples each time.
  3. Look at the pile of studies where there is no effect in the population.

Pre-lab activity questions:

  1. How much do the slope estimates vary between samples drawn from the same population?
  2. What happens to this variation when the samples are smaller or larger?
  3. In the pile of studies where there is no effect in the population, do all the slope estimates equal zero?

Keep your answers in mind for Steps 2 and 8 of the lab. When we look for an interaction, we compare slopes estimated in parts of a sample, so this variation matters.

Lab activity 1: What makes it easy or difficult for people to understand written health information?

Important

The research questions we are focussing on today are:

  1. What person attributes predict success in understanding health information?
  2. How can the effects of person attributes vary, in different groups of people, or for different values of a third variable (a moderator)?

This week, we work with a larger dataset. It combines the data from the two studies of adults in the UK, including the study two data you used in Weeks 7 and 8, with data collected from Lancaster first-year psychology students in a previous year. Step 2: Read in and inspect the data

The data file for this lab activity is all.data.csv. It has the same columns as the data file we used in Weeks 7 and 8:

  • participant_ID participant code;
  • mean.acc average accuracy of response to questions testing understanding of health guidance (varies between 0-1);
  • mean.self average self-rated accuracy of understanding of health guidance (varies between 1-9);
  • study variable coding for what study the data were collected in (study.one, study.two or PSYC122, the student data);
  • AGE age in years;
  • SHIPLEY vocabulary knowledge test score (varies between 0-40);
  • HLVA health literacy test score (varies between 1-16);
  • FACTOR3 reading strategy survey score (varies between 0-80);
  • GENDER gender code (here, Female or Male);
  • EDUCATION education level code (here, Further or Higher);
  • ETHNICITY ethnicity (Office National Statistics categories) code.

Action 1. In a new code chunk use read_csv() and here() to read in the data file. Give the data object a distinct name, e.g. all.data. Then use summary() to get an overview of the data.

TipCode 
all.data <- read_csv(here("4141_wk9_lab", "all.data.csv"))

summary(all.data)

In the summary, EDUCATION is shown as Class: character, so we cannot see how many participants are in each group. If we change it into a factor, summary() will count the participants at each level.

Action 2. Change EDUCATION into a factor using as.factor(). Then use summary() again.

TipCode 
all.data$EDUCATION <- as.factor(all.data$EDUCATION)

summary(all.data)
  • all.data$EDUCATION picks out the EDUCATION column of all.data.
  • as.factor() changes the variable into a factor, and the assignment arrow <- puts the result back in the same column.

Step 3: Visualise possible interactions

An interaction means that the effect of one predictor (here, the slope of the association between FACTOR3 and mean.acc) is different for different values of another variable. We can look for this by drawing the same scatterplot separately for different groups of participants.

Action 1. Draw a scatterplot of the association between FACTOR3 (x-axis) and mean.acc (y-axis), with a separate panel for each level of EDUCATION.

TipCode 
ggplot(data = all.data, aes(x = FACTOR3, y = mean.acc)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.5) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm") +
  theme_bw() +
  labs(x = "Reading strategy (FACTOR3)", y = "Mean accuracy") +
  xlim(0, 80) + ylim(0, 1) +
  facet_wrap(~ EDUCATION)
  • geom_smooth(method = "lm") adds a linear model line (the trend) with its 95% confidence interval as a grey band. R will show the message `geom_smooth()` using formula = 'y ~ x': this is not an error.
  • facet_wrap(~ EDUCATION) splits the data by EDUCATION and draws a separate panel for each level. See the facet_wrap() reference page.

To do the same for a numeric variable like HLVA, we first need to split the participants into groups based on their HLVA scores.

Action 2. Use cut_number() to split the participants into three groups of roughly equal size (low, mid and high HLVA scores). Then draw the scatterplot with a separate panel for each group.

TipCode 
all.data$HLVA_splits <- cut_number(all.data$HLVA, 3)

ggplot(data = all.data, aes(x = FACTOR3, y = mean.acc)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.5) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm") +
  theme_bw() +
  labs(x = "Reading strategy (FACTOR3)", y = "Mean accuracy") +
  xlim(0, 80) + ylim(0, 1) +
  facet_wrap(~ HLVA_splits, nrow = 1, labeller = label_both)
  • cut_number(all.data$HLVA, 3) orders the participants by their HLVA score and splits them into three groups. The result is stored in a new column, HLVA_splits. See the cut_number() reference page.
  • nrow = 1 puts the panels side by side, and labeller = label_both shows the variable name as well as the group in each panel label.
  • Where the splits fall depends on the scores in the sample.

Questions Step 3

  1. Compare the trend lines for Further and Higher education. Does the effect of reading strategy look different?
  2. Compare the trend lines for the three HLVA groups. What do the labels at the top of the panels (e.g. [2,8]) tell you, and how does the slope change from low to high HLVA?

Step 4: Centre numeric predictors and fit a model without an interaction

Before we fit models with interactions, we centre the numeric predictors. Centring a variable means subtracting the variable’s mean from every score, so that a centred score of 0 is the average score.

Why do this? In a model with an interaction, the estimate for each predictor on its own is its effect when the other predictor is 0. For our variables, 0 is not a useful value: nobody in the sample scored 0 on FACTOR3 (the lowest score is 9) or on HLVA (the lowest is 2). After centring, each estimate shows the effect for a person with an average score on the other predictor. Centring does not change the interaction estimate, its significance, or how well the model fits.

Action 1. In a new code chunk, create centred versions of FACTOR3 and HLVA.

TipCode 
all.data$FACTOR3_centered <- all.data$FACTOR3 - mean(all.data$FACTOR3, na.rm = TRUE)
all.data$HLVA_centered <- all.data$HLVA - mean(all.data$HLVA, na.rm = TRUE)
  • mean(all.data$FACTOR3, na.rm = TRUE) calculates the mean of FACTOR3. na.rm = TRUE tells R to ignore missing values, if there are any.
  • Subtracting the mean from the column gives the centred score for every participant, which we store in a new column.

Action 2. Fit a model with mean.acc as the outcome and the centred FACTOR3 and HLVA as predictors, without an interaction. Then get the model summary.

TipCode 
model.1 <- lm(mean.acc ~ FACTOR3_centered + HLVA_centered, data = all.data)
summary(model.1)

Questions Step 4

  1. What does a FACTOR3_centered score of 0 mean? What does a score of -10 mean?
  2. What does the intercept estimate mean in this model?
NoteHint
  • The intercept is the predicted outcome when all the predictors are 0.
  • Optional: fit the same model with the uncentred FACTOR3 and HLVA and compare the two summaries. What changes and what stays the same?

Step 5: Fit and plot an interaction between two numeric predictors

To include an interaction in a model, we separate the predictors with * instead of +:

lm(outcome ~ predictor.X*predictor.Z, data = data.set)

This gives a model with the effect of each predictor and the interaction between them. In the model summary, the interaction is shown as predictor.X:predictor.Z.

Action 1. In a new code chunk, fit a model with mean.acc as the outcome and the interaction between the centred FACTOR3 and HLVA. Then get the model summary.

TipCode 
model.2 <- lm(mean.acc ~ FACTOR3_centered * HLVA_centered, data = all.data)
summary(model.2)
NoteWhat do the estimates mean?
  • FACTOR3_centered: the effect (slope) of reading strategy for a person with an average HLVA score (HLVA_centered = 0).
  • HLVA_centered: the effect of health literacy for a person with an average FACTOR3 score.
  • FACTOR3_centered:HLVA_centered: the interaction. It shows how much the slope of FACTOR3 changes for each one-point increase in HLVA. A positive estimate means the reading strategy slope gets steeper as HLVA increases; a negative estimate means it gets shallower.

Interactions are easier to understand if we plot them. We use ggpredict(), as in Week 8, but now name two predictors in terms.

Action 2. Use ggpredict() and plot() to plot the predictions of the model for FACTOR3_centered, at different values of HLVA_centered.

TipCode 
dat.int.1 <- ggpredict(model.2, terms = c("FACTOR3_centered", "HLVA_centered"))
plot(dat.int.1) +
  theme_bw() +
  ylim(0, 1)
  • The first predictor named in terms goes on the x-axis. The second is the moderator: ggpredict() draws a separate line for three of its values, the mean (0) and one standard deviation below and above the mean (-2.19 and 2.19).
  • In the lecture slides, these plots are made with plot_model() from the sjPlot library. plot_model() uses ggpredict() to calculate the predictions, so the plots show the same thing.
  • The message Scale for y is already present is not an error: ylim() has replaced the y-axis that plot() set.

Questions Step 5

  1. Which estimate tells you about the interaction? Is the interaction significant?
  2. What does the sign of the interaction estimate tell you? Check your answer against the plot.
  3. Do the results support Theory 1 or Theory 2 from Pre-lab activity 1?

Step 6: Fit and plot an interaction between a numeric predictor and a categorical predictor

Interactions with a categorical predictor are coded in the same way.

Action 1. Fit a model with mean.acc as the outcome and the interaction between FACTOR3_centered and EDUCATION. Then get the model summary.

TipCode 
model.3 <- lm(mean.acc ~ FACTOR3_centered*EDUCATION, data = all.data)
summary(model.3)
NoteWhat do the estimates mean?

Further is the reference level (it comes first in the alphabet), so:

  • FACTOR3_centered: the slope of reading strategy for participants with Further education.
  • EDUCATIONHigher: the difference in predicted accuracy between Higher and Further education, for a person with an average FACTOR3 score.
  • FACTOR3_centered:EDUCATIONHigher: the interaction. It shows how much steeper (if positive) or shallower (if negative) the reading strategy slope is for Higher compared with Further education.

Action 2. Use ggpredict() and plot() to plot the predictions of the model for FACTOR3_centered, for each level of EDUCATION.

TipCode 
dat.int.2 <- ggpredict(model.3, terms = c("FACTOR3_centered", "EDUCATION"))
plot(dat.int.2) +
  theme_bw() +
  ylim(0, 1)

Questions Step 6

  1. Is the interaction significant?
  2. What is the slope of reading strategy for participants with Further education, and for participants with Higher education? Describe the interaction in words.
  3. What does the EDUCATIONHigher estimate mean, and is it significant?
NoteHint
  • For question b: the slope for Higher education is the slope for Further education plus the interaction estimate.
  • For question d: what value of reading strategy does the EDUCATIONHigher estimate refer to in each model?

We fitted a linear model with mean comprehension accuracy as the outcome and reading strategy (FACTOR3, centred on its mean), education level and their interaction as predictors. Step 8: Extension – How robust is the interaction?

Look again at the Further panel of the first plot in Step 3. One participant has a reading strategy score of 9, far below everyone else (the next lowest score is 29), and high accuracy (0.95). In Week 5, you looked at how unusual data points can affect a correlation. The same can happen in a linear model.

Action: Fit the model from Step 6 again, without this participant. Then get the model summary.

TipCode 
all.data.check <- filter(all.data, FACTOR3 > 9)

model.4 <- lm(mean.acc ~ FACTOR3_centered * EDUCATION, data = all.data.check)
summary(model.4)
  • filter(all.data, FACTOR3 > 9) keeps only the rows (participants) for whom FACTOR3 is greater than 9, and we store the result as a new dataset, all.data.check. The original all.data is not changed.

Questions Step 8

  1. Is the interaction still significant without this participant? How has the interaction estimate changed?
  2. What does this tell you about how much confidence to place in the interaction? Should we remove this participant from the analysis?
